DARVO, Blame-Shifting, and Playing the Victim: A Clear Explanation

GenZ Frontier Editorial | August 15, 2026

DARVO stands for Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender. The term describes a response in which a person accused of wrongdoing denies the conduct, attacks the credibility or character of the person raising the concern, and presents themselves as the real victim.

DARVO is useful as a pattern-recognition concept, but it is often overused online. A denial is not automatically DARVO. A person can reject a false accusation, remember events differently, feel hurt, or explain their side without attacking the accuser or reversing responsibility. Identifying a DARVO-like response does not prove that the original allegation is true, and it does not determine legal guilt.

This guide explains the sequence, what research suggests about how it can affect observers, how it differs from ordinary defensiveness, and how to protect your clarity and safety when accountability is being redirected.

Safety note: If a disclosure involves sexual violence, domestic abuse, stalking, workplace retaliation, threats, or institutional reporting, seek confidential support from a qualified advocate, professional, or appropriate authority. Do not rely on an internet label as a substitute for evidence or safety planning.

The Short Answer

A DARVO-like response may include three linked moves:

  1. Deny: “That did not happen,” or “I did nothing wrong.”
  2. Attack: “You are unstable, dishonest, vindictive, or impossible to please.”
  3. Reverse victim and offender: “I am the one being abused,” while the original concern is abandoned.

The pattern is more concerning when it is repeated, paired with intimidation or reputation attacks, and used to avoid accountability. It is not a test that proves who is telling the truth.

What Does DARVO Stand For?

Letter Meaning Common pattern
D Deny Deny the conduct, the effect, or the existence of the concern
A Attack Attack the accuser’s character, memory, motives, credibility, or mental health
RVO Reverse Victim and Offender Present the accused person as the victim and the accuser as the wrongdoer

The acronym describes a response pattern, not a personality type. It was developed in discussions of how people who commit wrongdoing may respond when confronted. Contemporary research is still limited, and the term should be applied carefully.

DARVO in a Fictional Example

Imagine a fictional workplace scenario. An employee tells a supervisor, “You changed my schedule after I objected, and I have the written messages.” The supervisor responds:

  • Deny: “I never changed anything.”
  • Attack: “You are unreliable and trying to create drama.”
  • Reverse: “I am the one being harassed by you. You are the problem.”

This example illustrates the structure, not a conclusion about what actually happened. A responsible evaluation would review the messages, schedule, witnesses, policies, and each person’s account independently.

How DARVO Can Affect Observers

A 2023 study by Sarah Harsey and Jennifer Freyd examined how DARVO affected observers’ judgments in fictional sexual-assault scenarios.1 The study included 230 undergraduate participants. People exposed to a perpetrator using DARVO rated that person as less abusive, less responsible, and more believable. They also rated the fictional victim as more abusive and less believable.

The study suggests that deny-attack-reversal can influence how observers interpret an allegation. It does not show that every denial is DARVO, that every person using the pattern committed the alleged conduct, or that the pattern alone proves a case.

The practical lesson is to evaluate the original behavior and the response separately. A counter-accusation should not automatically erase the initial complaint, and the initial complaint should not automatically prove every counter-accusation false.

DARVO vs. Ordinary Defense

Response What it may look like Why it is not automatically DARVO
Denial “I did not do that.” The allegation may be false, mistaken, or disputed
Clarification “That is not how I remember the agreement.” Memory and interpretation can differ in good faith
Accountability “I did that, and I understand why it hurt you.” Acknowledgment and repair move toward responsibility
Defensiveness “I feel attacked by this conversation.” Poor emotional regulation is not the same as role reversal
Counter-complaint “I also experienced harmful behavior.” The counter-claim may be genuine and should be evaluated independently
DARVO-like pattern Denial plus personal attack plus reversal of victim and offender The linked pattern shifts attention away from the original conduct

A person may be defensive and still later listen, provide evidence, acknowledge impact, or participate in a fair process. A DARVO-like pattern is more concerning when it consistently prevents the original issue from being discussed.

Common DARVO Patterns

Denying the conduct

The person may deny the event, minimize what happened, deny the effect, or insist that the concern is too vague to matter. Denial can be appropriate when an allegation is false. The warning pattern is not denial by itself; it is denial combined with attacks, intimidation, and reversal.

Attacking credibility

The person may call the accuser crazy, jealous, vindictive, confused, unstable, attention-seeking, or dishonest. They may bring up unrelated mistakes, private information, past trauma, or character flaws to make observers focus on identity rather than conduct.

A person’s imperfections do not automatically disprove a specific report. Evidence should be assessed rather than replaced with a character contest.

Reversing the roles

The accused person may present themselves as the only injured party and describe the person raising the concern as the abuser. The reversal can happen through private conversations, social media, workplace reports, family pressure, or legal processes.

WomensLaw describes blame shifting as denying abuse, blaming the survivor for the abuser’s actions, or making the survivor appear to be the bad one. It gives examples such as spreading lies, claiming that the survivor is the abusive one, or making a false counter-report.2

These are serious possibilities, but an article cannot determine whether a specific report is false. Each claim requires independent evaluation.

Recruiting an audience

The person may tell friends, coworkers, relatives, or community members a selective story before the original concern can be understood. They may use public posts, group messages, or private calls to obtain sympathy and pressure the accuser.

Seeking support is not itself DARVO. A person who is accused may need support, too. The concern is whether the communication is used to silence, intimidate, isolate, or punish the person who raised the issue.

DARVO and Apologies

An apology can be sincere, incomplete, defensive, or manipulative. A useful apology generally identifies the conduct, acknowledges impact, avoids “but you made me,” and includes a realistic plan to repair.

An apology may be part of a blame-shifting cycle when it says, “I am sorry you felt that way,” then attacks the person for bringing up the harm, demands immediate forgiveness, or treats the apology as proof that the issue must never be discussed again.

The absence of an apology does not prove DARVO, and the presence of an apology does not prove accountability. Look at whether behavior changes consistently.

What to Do When a Conversation Turns Into DARVO-Like Blame Shifting

You do not need to win the argument or force an admission. Your immediate goal may be to preserve clarity, boundaries, and safety.

Try separating the original concern from the counter-claim:

“I am willing to discuss your concern separately. I am still asking about what happened in the original incident.”

“I will not debate my entire character. I am discussing the specific behavior and its impact.”

“A counter-accusation does not answer the question I raised. We can address it through an appropriate process.”

“I am ending this conversation because it has become threatening. I will communicate through a safer channel.”

These statements are options, not guaranteed scripts. If the person has power over your housing, money, employment, children, reputation, or physical safety, consider support before confronting them.

Protecting Evidence and Perspective

If it is safe, keep important messages, dates, agreements, or reports in a place the other person cannot easily alter or access. Documentation can help a qualified professional or appropriate authority evaluate a pattern. But devices and accounts may be monitored, and secret recording laws vary.

Do not publish private messages, threaten exposure, create a public campaign, or bait someone into a response. If the issue is high-risk, confidential safety planning is more important than proving a label online.

What DARVO Does Not Prove

DARVO does not prove that the original accusation is true. It does not prove that a person has a personality disorder. It does not prove that a counter-complaint is false. It does not replace evidence, due process, clinical assessment, workplace investigation, or legal advice.

It can still be useful to recognize how denial, attack, and role reversal affect attention and credibility. The safest practice is to evaluate each claim independently and notice whether intimidation or social pressure is being used to prevent fair evaluation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does DARVO stand for?

DARVO stands for Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender. It describes a response pattern in which a person denies wrongdoing, attacks the person raising the concern, and presents themselves as the real victim.

Is DARVO a diagnosis?

No. DARVO is an acronym and a framework for describing a communication pattern. It is not a psychiatric diagnosis, personality type, or legal finding.

Does DARVO prove someone is guilty?

No. DARVO-like behavior can affect how observers interpret a claim, but it does not prove the underlying conduct. Evidence about the original behavior must be evaluated independently.

Is denying an accusation DARVO?

Not by itself. A person may deny a false or misunderstood accusation. DARVO refers to a linked pattern involving denial, attacks on the accuser, and reversal of victim and offender roles.

Is playing the victim always DARVO?

No. A person can genuinely be hurt, mistreated, or falsely accused. The concern is whether someone uses a victim role to avoid accountability, attack the other person, and redirect attention from the original conduct.

Is blame-shifting the same as DARVO?

Blame-shifting is broader. DARVO is one pattern of blame-shifting that specifically combines denial, attack, and role reversal. Not every attempt to explain context or raise a separate concern is DARVO.

What should I do if someone DARVOs me?

Stay focused on specific behavior, separate the original issue from any counter-claim, preserve evidence safely, set communication limits, and seek qualified support. If threats or retaliation are present, prioritize safety planning rather than confrontation.

Can a victim use DARVO?

Anyone can deny, attack, or reverse roles in a conversation. The label should not be used to decide who is a victim without evaluating the underlying facts. Describe the behavior and allow each claim to be assessed independently.

Final Takeaway

DARVO describes a response pattern: deny the conduct, attack the person raising the concern, and reverse the roles of victim and offender. Research suggests that this pattern can influence observers’ judgments and make a person who reports harm seem less credible.1

Use the term carefully. DARVO does not prove guilt, and a denial does not prove innocence or manipulation. Keep the original behavior, the response, the evidence, and the safety context separate. When accountability turns into threats, reputation attacks, or retaliation, seek support rather than trying to win a label-based argument.
